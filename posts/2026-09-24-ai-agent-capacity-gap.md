Two-thirds of companies plan to invest in AI agents within the next year. Only 10 percent run mostly autonomous operations today. The 56-point gap between ambition and execution is not a technology problem — it is a capacity problem.

A study commissioned by IFS and published through Inside Telecom found that manufacturers are the most enthusiastic adopters and also the most stretched. The companies that move past pilots are not the ones that bought better models. They are the ones that built internal capacity to operate what they build.

A 50-person services firm feels the same pressure from a different angle. Your team handles client delivery, pipeline, and overhead on a schedule that leaves no slack for experimentation. You need agents that handle intake, triage, and follow-up. But you have no one on staff who can design, test, and maintain them without pulling people off the revenue line.

Most AI projects stall on adoption, not the model. You can buy a demo that works in a sandbox and watch it fail the moment it touches real client data or a real deadline. The bottleneck is never the intelligence — it is the bandwidth to integrate that intelligence into workflows your team actually uses.

The companies that close the gap do three things differently.

They start with the workflow, not the agent. They map where work handoffs break, where people re-enter data, where context gets lost — and they build the smallest automated step that fixes one of those breaks. One working step ships in weeks, not quarters.

They design for human review from day one. The agent handles the repetitive part. A person reviews the output before it goes anywhere that matters. That structure builds trust without slowing things down.

They build internal muscle. This is where most vendors stop and walk away. The teams that sustain agent use across their organization pair whatever they ship with hands-on enablement so the people who do the work understand how the agent behaves, when it is reliable, and when to take back control.

That pairing — building the system and building the capacity to operate it — is what turns a six-month pilot into a working practice.
